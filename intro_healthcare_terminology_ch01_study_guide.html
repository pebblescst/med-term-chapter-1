Chapter 1 is the foundation of the entire course. Rather than memorizing individual clinical terms, you are learning how the language works — the code that unlocks thousands of medical terms. Master the CARD method, the 5 word-building rules, and the suffix categories here and every chapter becomes easier.
What Chapter 1 covers
Concept
Word structure
Every medical term is built from up to four parts: word root (core meaning), combining vowel (usually "o"), prefix (modifies, precedes), and suffix (follows, defines type). Word root + combining vowel = combining form.
Method
CARD method
Check word parts → Assign meanings → Reverse suffix to front → Define. Example: glossitis → gloss/o (tongue) + -itis (inflammation) → inflammation of the tongue.
Concept
Types of terms
Decodable: 90% of terms — built from Greek/Latin parts. Nondecodable: 10% — memorize whole (cataract, asthma). Eponyms: named for a person (Alzheimer disease). Acronyms: letters of a phrase (AIDS).
Clinical
Diagnosis vs prognosis
Diagnosis: PRESENT condition — what the patient has NOW. Prognosis: FUTURE condition — expected outcome. Pro- = before; -gnosis = state of knowledge. Prognosis = knowledge ahead of time.
Clinical
Signs vs symptoms
Signs: OBJECTIVE — observable/measurable (fever, rash, elevated BP). Symptoms: SUBJECTIVE — reported by patient, not directly measurable ("I'm dizzy," pain). Pain is ALWAYS a symptom.
Clinical
Acute vs chronic
Acute: sudden onset, short-term, sharp (acu- = sharp). Begins severely and resolves. Chronic: long-duration, recurring nature. chron/o = time. Chronic diseases persist and return over months to years.
Concept
Four categories of terms
(1) Anatomy & physiology — structure/function. (2) Pathology — diseases/disorders. (3) Diagnostic procedures — tests. (4) Therapeutic interventions — treatments. The suffix usually signals the category.
Language
Greek & Latin origins
90% of medical terms derive from Greek and Latin. Many structures have both a Greek and Latin combining form. Tongue: Latin lingu/o (bilingual, sublingual); Greek gloss/o (glossary, glossitis). Both appear in medical terminology.
Term anatomy at a glance
Word root
The core meaning of a term. Cannot stand alone. Examples: gloss (tongue), gastr (stomach), arthr (joint). The slash in a combining form shows where the root ends: gloss/ + o = gloss/o.
Combining vowel
Usually "o" — links the word root to a suffix or another combining form. DROPPED if suffix starts with a vowel. KEPT if suffix starts with a consonant. ALWAYS kept between two combining forms.
Combining form
Word root + combining vowel, written with a slash: gloss/o, gastr/o, arthr/o. The "subject" of the medical term — what the term is about.
Prefix
Attached to the BEGINNING of a term. No combining vowel needed. Modifies meaning: absence, location, number/quantity, or state. May have more than one meaning.
Suffix
Attached to the END of a term. Defines the term's TYPE/category. When defining, the suffix meaning comes FIRST (Reverse step in CARD). Nearly always present in medical terms.

Key terms — defined
TermDefinition & clinical notes
Word rootThe core meaning component of a medical term. The foundation on which the term is built. Not used alone — needs a combining vowel, suffix, or prefix. Examples: gloss (tongue), gastr (stomach), arthr (joint).
Combining formWord root + combining vowel (usually "o"). Written with a slash: gloss/o, gastr/o, arthr/o. The SUBJECT of the term — what it is about. The slash shows where the word root ends and the combining vowel begins.
PrefixA word part placed BEFORE the word root. Modifies the term's meaning by indicating absence, location, number/quantity, or state. Attached directly — no combining vowel needed. May have more than one meaning. Example: hypo- + gloss/o = hypoglossal (below the tongue).
SuffixA word part placed AFTER the combining form or word root. Defines the term's type or category. When defining a term using CARD, the suffix meaning comes FIRST (Reverse). Example: gloss/o + -itis = glossitis (inflammation of the tongue).
Decodable termA medical term built from recognizable Greek/Latin word parts that CAN be decoded by applying the CARD method. Approximately 90% of medical terms. Example: gastralgia (gastr/o + -algia = pain of the stomach).
Nondecodable termA medical term that CANNOT be broken into meaningful component parts — must be memorized whole. Approximately 10% of terms. Examples: cataract (Greek "waterfall" — not medically useful), asthma. Greek/Latin origins exist but do not reveal the medical meaning.
AcronymAn abbreviation formed from the FIRST LETTERS of a series of words, pronounced as a word. Examples: AIDS (Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome), ADHD. Distinct from simple abbreviations — acronyms are spoken as words.
EponymA medical term carrying the name of the person or place associated with the discovery or condition. Examples: Alzheimer disease (Alois Alzheimer), Achilles tendon, Parkinson disease, Lou Gehrig disease, fallopian tubes, cesarean section. Eponyms are nondecodable.
DiagnosisThe identification of a patient's PRESENT condition. From Greek: dia- (through) + -gnosis (knowledge) = complete knowledge of the condition. Abbreviated Dx. The diagnostic workup = the process of determining the diagnosis.
PrognosisThe prediction of a patient's FUTURE condition — expected outcome or course of a disease. From Greek: pro- (before) + -gnosis (knowledge) = knowledge ahead of time. Abbreviated Px. Example: "The prognosis is favorable."
SignAn OBJECTIVE clinical finding — observable and/or measurable by a healthcare provider. Examples: fever (measured), rash (seen), elevated blood pressure, abnormal lab value. Opposite of symptom.
SymptomA SUBJECTIVE complaint — experienced by the patient but not directly observable or measurable. Must be reported by the patient. Examples: "I feel dizzy," "I have pain." Pain is ALWAYS a symptom. Opposite of sign.
AcuteDescribing a disease with sudden onset, short duration, and sharp/severe intensity. From Latin: acu- = sharp (as in acupuncture). Acute conditions begin abruptly and resolve. Example: acute appendicitis, acute myocardial infarction.
ChronicDescribing a disease of long duration that recurs or persists without full resolution. From Greek: chron/o = time. Example: chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), chronic low back pain.
CARD method — decoding medical terms
C
Check
Identify all word parts in the term. Look for the combining form and suffix (and prefix if present).
A
Assign
Assign a meaning to each word part using your word part knowledge.
R
Reverse
Put the suffix meaning FIRST, before the combining form meaning. The suffix leads the definition.
D
Define
State the complete definition of the term in natural language.
CARD worked example: gastralgia
C: gastr/o + -algia  |  A: gastr/o = stomach · -algia = pain  |  R: pain / stomach  |  D: pain of the stomach

Is this a sign or a symptom? → Symptom — pain cannot be objectively measured or observed; it is reported by the patient.
CARD worked example: arthritis
C: arthr/o + -itis (dropping "o" because -itis starts with a vowel)  |  A: arthr/o = joint · -itis = inflammation  |  R: inflammation / joint  |  D: inflammation of a joint

Note: arthr + -itis = arthritis (NOT "arthroitis") — combining vowel dropped per Word Building Rule #1.
Term category identification
Category 1
Anatomy & physiology
Terms describing STRUCTURE and FUNCTION. Suffixes: -al, -um, -ic, -ar (all mean "pertaining to"). Examples: cardiac (pertaining to the heart), endocardium (membrane within the heart), gastric (pertaining to the stomach).
Category 2
Pathology
Terms describing DISEASES and DISORDERS. Key suffixes: -itis (inflammation), -algia (pain), -emia (blood condition), -malacia (softening), -megaly (enlargement), -ptosis (drooping), -sclerosis (hardening), -stenosis (narrowing).
Category 3
Diagnostic procedures
Terms describing TESTS and EXAMINATIONS. Key suffixes: -graphy (process of recording), -scopy (visual examination), -metry (process of measuring), -opsy (view of tissue). Instruments: -scope, -graph, -meter.
Category 4
Therapeutic interventions
Terms describing TREATMENTS and SURGERIES. Key suffixes: -ectomy (removal), -plasty (surgical repair), -stomy (new opening), -tomy (incision), -tripsy (crushing).

Critical look-alike prefix pairs
ante- vs anti-
ante- = BEFORE (time/position): antepartum, anteversion. anti- = AGAINST (opposing): antibacterial, anticoagulant. "anti-" fights AGAINST something.
hyper- vs hypo-
hyper- = ABOVE NORMAL / EXCESSIVE: hypertension, hyperglycemia. hypo- = BELOW NORMAL / DEFICIENT: hypotension, hypoglycemia. These are OPPOSITES — most commonly tested prefix pair.
per- vs peri-
per- = THROUGH (from one side to other): percutaneous (through the skin). peri- = AROUND / SURROUNDING (encircling): pericardium. The extra "i" in peri- = it surrounds/encircles.
inter- vs intra-
inter- = BETWEEN (two separate things): intervertebral. intra- = WITHIN (inside one thing): intradermal. "intra" = "intro" = into = inside.
pre- vs ante- vs pro-
All mean "before" but used in specific contexts. pre- = before (surgical context): prenatal, preoperative. ante- = before (position): antepartum. pro- = forward/ahead of (prognosis).
sub- vs hypo-
Both mean "below/under." sub- = Latin origin: sublingual, subcutaneous. hypo- = Greek origin: hypoglossal, hypoglycemia. Same meaning, different linguistic roots.

Combining forms with dual meanings or common confusion
ped/o — child OR foot
Derives from both Greek pais (child) AND Latin pes/pedis (foot). Pediatrics/pediatrician = child medicine. Pedal, pedometer = foot. The specialist for feet uses pod/o (podiatrist). Always determine meaning from context and paired word parts.
lingu/o vs gloss/o — both = tongue
Latin lingu/o: bilingual (two languages), sublingual (under tongue). Greek gloss/o: glossary (list of terms), glossitis (tongue inflammation). Both appear in medical terminology. gloss/o is more common in pathology; lingu/o in anatomy/adjective forms.
-malacia vs -megaly
Both begin with "m" and are pathology suffixes. -malacia = SOFTENING (chondromalacia, osteomalacia). -megaly = ENLARGEMENT (hepatomegaly, splenomegaly). These are completely different conditions — a very common student error on exams.
-graphy vs -scopy vs -opsy
All diagnostic suffixes: -graphy = process of RECORDING/IMAGING (mammography). -scopy = visual EXAMINATION (colonoscopy). -opsy = examination of REMOVED TISSUE (biopsy). Each signals a different type of diagnostic procedure.
The 5 rules for building medical terms govern how word parts are joined. Master these rules and you can correctly build — and spell — any medical term from its component parts.
The 5 word-building rules
Rule 1
If the suffix starts with a VOWEL → drop the combining vowel
The combining vowel "o" is NOT needed when the suffix already starts with a vowel. Think: "two vowels in a row = too much glue." The suffix provides the linking vowel.
arthr/o + -itis = arthritis (NOT arthroitis) — "o" dropped; -itis starts with vowel "i" gloss/o + -itis = glossitis (NOT glossoitis) — same rule
Rule 2
If the suffix starts with a CONSONANT → keep the combining vowel
When the suffix begins with a consonant, the combining vowel is needed to link the parts and make pronunciation possible.
arthr/o + -plasty = arthroplasty — "o" KEPT; -plasty starts with "p" (consonant) gastr/o + -logy = gastrology — "o" kept; -logy starts with "l" (consonant)
Rule 3
If a combining form ends with the same vowel that begins the suffix → drop one vowel
When the last letter of a word root and the first letter of a suffix are the same vowel, drop one to avoid duplication.
endocardi/o + -itis = endocarditis (NOT endocardioitis) — "i" ends root + "i" begins -itis → drop one
Rule 4
When two or more combining forms are joined → always keep the combining vowel between them
The vowel/consonant rule (Rules 1 & 2) applies ONLY to suffixes. Between two combining forms, always keep the combining vowel — regardless of whether the second form starts with a vowel or consonant.
gastr/o + enter/o + -itis = gastroenteritis — "o" kept between two CFs; "o" dropped before -itis
Rule 5
When multiple combining forms are used → order them by anatomical sequence or medical logic
The order of combining forms follows the natural anatomical pathway or standard medical convention. The order must match the path through the body.
esophag/o + gastr/o + duoden/o + -scopy = esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) Order follows the anatomical route: esophagus → stomach → duodenum (correct direction of GI tract)
